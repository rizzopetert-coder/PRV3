Employment
Litigation Taxonomy

The most financially precise lens in the series. Litigation categories aren't opinions or survey responses — they are organizational conditions that a court, a regulator, or a plaintiff's attorney has already named, documented, and attached a dollar value to. The causal chain from organizational condition to financial consequence is established by law, not inferred from research.

This experiment surfaces conditions the organization was most motivated to conceal. Reviews describe what employees were willing to say publicly. Exit interviews describe what employees were willing to say on record. Litigation describes what the organization did when nobody was supposed to find out — and did anyway.

What the Litigation Reveals
Harassment claims cluster in organizations with three structural characteristics: management trained in prevention without infrastructure to detect or respond; a reporting process employees don't trust; and a cultural norm where the behavior has been present long enough to be normalized. New employees observe it, conclude it's acceptable, and the organization loses the ability to self-correct.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Normalization Creep — the gradual drift in what the organization treats as acceptable. Behavior challenged in year one, tolerated in year three, invisible in year seven. By the time litigation surfaces it, the behavior has been present long enough that multiple managers, HR representatives, and senior leaders have encountered it without acting. The litigation names the behavior. The condition is the accumulated tolerance that made it possible.
Financial Consequence
$75K–$125K average direct settlement plus $100K–$300K legal fees before verdict. Reputational damage — measured by Glassdoor rating decline, employer brand impact on recruiting costs, and voluntary attrition in aftermath — adds organizational costs beyond the direct legal settlement that standard financial models do not capture.

Claim Category
Discrimination — Hiring, Promotion, and Termination
EEOC Recovery
$665M FY2023 (record)
Largest Settlements
$40.6M–$175M (class)
Legal Mechanism
Disparate impact
What the Litigation Reveals
Discrimination claims in hiring cluster in organizations without structured interview frameworks, documented selection criteria, or diverse interview panels. Promotion claims cluster where advancement criteria are subjective, undocumented, and applied inconsistently. Termination claims cluster in three patterns: following protected activity, with absent documentation, and in reductions where selection criteria produce demographic disparate impact.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Disparate Impact Architecture — facially neutral policies that produce systematically discriminatory outcomes through their operation rather than individual intent. Promotion criteria, performance rating systems, and reduction selection matrices designed without disparate impact analysis. The organization doesn't intend discrimination. The design produces it. Under Title VII, intent is not required — the outcome and the absence of business necessity are sufficient.
Financial Consequence
EEOC recovered $665M for discrimination claimants in FY2023 — the highest in agency history. Class action disparate impact cases produce the largest individual values: Beck v. Boeing ($40.6M–$72.5M, gender discrimination class action), Novartis gender discrimination ($175M). Individual verdict exposure: Tilkey v. Allstate — jury awarded $18.6M (wrongful termination + defamation); wrongful termination reversed on appeal, defamation affirmed, final outcome ~$4M. Systematic Disparate Impact Architecture produces class action exposure — the entire affected population becomes the claim.
Conditions Surfaced
New Candidate
Disparate Impact Architecture
Organizational systems designed without disparate impact analysis that produce discriminatory outcomes through their operation. The strongest financial consequence narrative in the taxonomy — class action exposure with no statutory cap.

Claim Category
Wage and Hour Violations
DOL Recovery
$259M FY2025
Cost Multiplier
2x back wages (federal); some states separately permit treble damages under state law
Discovery Method
Rarely internal
What the Litigation Reveals
Wage and hour claims cluster around FLSA misclassification, off-the-clock work, and meal and rest break violations. The critical diagnostic characteristic: violations are almost never discovered internally. They surface through employee complaints, DOL audits, or class action attorneys who specialize in identifying industries with systematic violations. By the time litigation begins, violations typically span years and affect hundreds or thousands of employees.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Compliance Normalization — illegal practices present long enough to become the operational norm. Not ignorance of the law — often the organization knows the practice is technically non-compliant but has made an implicit calculation that the risk is manageable. The off-the-clock work is tolerated. The misclassification was reviewed once and left in place. The break policy exists on paper and is routinely violated in practice.
Financial Consequence
DOL recovered $259M in back wages in FY2025. The more consequential 2025 development is structural, not numerical: WHD stopped seeking liquidated damages in pre-litigation administrative settlements as of mid-2025, and relaunched its PAID self-audit program allowing voluntary disclosure without penalty. The liquidated-damages multiplier — 2x under federal law; some states separately permit treble damages under state law — still applies if an employee pursues litigation; it no longer applies to the administrative path most employers actually face.
Conditions Surfaced
New Candidate
Compliance Normalization
Illegal practices normalized through extended tolerance. Not ignorance — an implicit calculation that the risk is manageable. The organization knows. It continues. The liability accumulates silently until external discovery.

Regulatory Exposure

04
Claim Category
Retaliation
EEOC Volume
56% of all charges FY2023
Verdict Profile
Highest in employment law
Punitive Exposure
Elevated — perceived intent
What the Litigation Reveals
Retaliation claims arise from a sequence of decisions, not a single bad one. The complaint, the investigation, the outcome, and the treatment of the complainant after. Most common sequence: employee complains → HR concludes unsubstantiated → complainant subsequently passed over for promotion, receives negative review, or included in RIF. The organization treats each decision as independent. The court treats the sequence as retaliation.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Sequential Decision Blindness — individual decision-makers act in isolation without awareness of how their decisions appear in sequence. The promotion decision-maker doesn't know about the complaint. The review manager doesn't know about the promotion decision. The RIF selector doesn't connect to the prior complaint. No individual acted with retaliatory intent. The sequence constitutes retaliation. The absence of a coordinated oversight mechanism makes this structurally inevitable.
Financial Consequence
Retaliation verdicts are consistently the highest in employment law. Juarez v. AutoZone (2014): individual plaintiff verdict, pregnancy discrimination and retaliation — post-verdict confidential settlement July 2015, amount undisclosed. Shoots v. Publix Super Markets (2014): ~90,650-member FCRA background-check class action — reclassified under FCRA statutory class action liability, not retaliation. Punitive damage exposure in retaliation cases is higher than in underlying discrimination cases because juries perceive retaliation as deliberate — even when it results from Sequential Decision Blindness rather than intent. The organization pays for intent it didn't have.

Claim Category
FMLA and ADA Interference and Retaliation
ADA Charges FY2023
26,000+
Cost Multiplier
2x economic loss (liquidated damages double back pay by default under 29 U.S.C. § 2617(a)(1)(A)(iii) unless employer proves good faith)
Root Cause
Manager-level decisions
What the Litigation Reveals
FMLA and ADA claims cluster in organizations where leave administration is handled by direct managers rather than HR — producing inconsistent and legally indefensible decisions. The most diagnostic characteristic: organizations producing these claims at elevated rates are almost always those where managers make leave and accommodation decisions independently, because HR infrastructure is absent, captured, or inaccessible. The decisions are individually understandable and collectively catastrophic.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Manager Legal Overreach — direct managers routinely making employment decisions that legally require HR involvement, legal review, or a structured interactive process. FMLA designation, ADA accommodation, termination during protected leave, performance management of employees with disclosed disabilities — all require processes that managers bypass because they don't know the process exists, HR isn't available, or the culture normalizes manager autonomy in legally constrained decisions.
Financial Consequence
Average ADA settlement: $75K–$150K. FMLA violations produce back pay, liquidated damages equal to back pay, and attorney fees — liquidated damages double back pay by default; courts award them unless the employer proves good faith and reasonable grounds. Unlike FLSA, FMLA claims are rarely resolved administratively, so this doubling is the norm rather than an escalation from a lower administrative baseline. The condition — managers making legally constrained decisions independently — is both highly prevalent and highly preventable, making the financial consequence narrative particularly direct: the cost of legal training and HR process access is a fraction of the cost of a single ADA or FMLA claim.
Conditions Surfaced
New Candidate
Manager Legal Overreach
Direct managers making legally constrained employment decisions independently. Not malicious — they don't know the process exists or HR is unavailable. The decisions feel reasonable. They're legally indefensible.

Wrongful Termination
Settlement Range
$50K–Several million
Primary Weakness
Documentation deficit
High-Risk Jurisdictions

What the Litigation Reveals
Wrongful termination claims cluster around two structural failures: documentation deficit (termination for performance without the documentation trail establishing the problem, notice, and opportunity to improve) and selection inconsistency (termination criteria applied inconsistently across demographic groups). The most consistent finding at deposition: the manager genuinely believes the termination is justified. The file doesn't support the belief.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Invisible Performance Management — performance expectations, feedback, and consequences communicated verbally and managed relationally rather than documented formally. The manager knows who is performing and who isn't. The file doesn't reflect it. The termination decision is accurate. The documentation is indefensible. Distinct from Unmanaged Underperformance — that's tolerance of performance problems. This is managing performance in a way that produces legally indefensible decisions even when the underlying judgment is correct.
Financial Consequence
Wrongful termination settlements range from $50K to several million depending on jurisdiction, protected class involved, and egregiousness. California, New York, and New Jersey produce the highest values. Plaintiffs' attorneys selectively pursue cases with strong documentation deficits — the cases where Invisible Performance Management is most evident. Organizations with this condition are disproportionately targeted because they're disproportionately winnable.
Conditions Surfaced
New Candidate
Invisible Performance Management
Performance managed through relationship and conversation rather than documentation. The manager's judgment is accurate. The file doesn't support it. The termination is legally indefensible not because it's wrong but because it's undocumented.
Confirms Existing

Claim Category
Whistleblower and Retaliation for Protected Activity
SEC Awards Since 2012
$1.9B+ total
Largest Single Award
$279M
Statutory Cap
None (Dodd-Frank)
What the Litigation Reveals
Whistleblower claims arise almost exclusively in organizations where the internal reporting infrastructure failed first. The employee who goes to the SEC, the DOL, or the state attorney general almost always went to HR or management first. The external report is the response to an internal failure. By the time the external report arrives, the organization has both the underlying violation and the retaliatory response to the internal report.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Internal Reporting Failure — the internal reporting mechanism exists, employees use it, and it fails to produce either remediation or protection for the reporter. Distinct from Misconduct Infrastructure Absence (mechanism doesn't exist) and HR Capture (mechanism is compromised). This is specifically a mechanism that functions well enough to receive reports and fails at remediation and protection. The employee's experience: I reported. The report was taken seriously. Nothing changed. I faced consequences. I have no choice but to go external.
Financial Consequence
SEC whistleblower awards have no statutory cap — Dodd-Frank pays 10–30% of sanctions over $1M. Largest individual award: $279M. For the organization, the SEC investigation triggered by a whistleblower report produces sanctions, remediation costs, and reputational damage that dwarf any cost of fixing the underlying condition. Internal Reporting Failure is the most extreme financial consequence narrative in the taxonomy — the gap between the cost of fixing the internal mechanism and the cost of external exposure is orders of magnitude.

08
Claim Category
NLRA Violations and Union Activity
NLRB Trajectory
Charges increasing
Primary Risk
Organizing success
Root Cause
Manager suppression
What the Litigation Reveals
NLRA violations cluster in organizations where managers treat discussion of wages, working conditions, and organizing as insubordination — violating Section 7 protected activity rights that cover these conversations. The diagnostic characteristic: organizations producing NLRA violations at elevated rates have almost always trained managers to suppress rather than address employee concerns. The underlying concerns are legitimate. The response is illegal.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Organizing Trigger Blindness — management doesn't understand that specific employee concerns about workload, compensation, and management behavior are protected discussions under the NLRA, and responds to them in ways that constitute interference with protected activity. Distinct from a deliberate anti-union campaign — this condition is about acting illegally because management doesn't know the legal framework governing its response to employee concerns.
Financial Consequence
NLRB violations produce back pay, reinstatement orders, and remedial posting requirements. More significantly, organizations that mishandle organizing activity often produce the organizing success they were trying to prevent — the mishandling becomes organizing fuel. The cost of a unionized workforce in an industry where competitors are non-union is organization-specific but consistently significant. The financial consequence of Organizing Trigger Blindness is the organizing outcome it accelerates.
Conditions Surfaced
New Candidate
Organizing Trigger Blindness
Management acts illegally in response to organizing activity because it doesn't understand the legal framework governing its response. Not deliberate anti-union strategy — ignorance of protected activity rights producing illegal interference.
Confirms Existing
Organizational Deafness
Confirmed. The underlying employee concerns that drive organizing — workload, compensation, management behavior — are the conditions Organizational Deafness fails to detect and address before they become organizing motivation.

Claim Category
AI Bias and Algorithmic Discrimination
Status
Emerging — first class actions
NYC LL144 Fines
$500–$1,500/violation
Class Action Trajectory
Rapidly increasing
What the Litigation Reveals
AI bias claims cluster in organizations that deployed AI tools in hiring, performance, or workforce planning without understanding that the tools' outputs constitute employment decisions for legal purposes. The organization believes it's using a tool. The law treats it as making an employment decision. The EEOC has made clear that Title VII applies to AI-assisted decisions. The first class action AI bias claims in employment are working through the courts.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Algorithmic Accountability Gap — the organization can't explain its own AI-assisted decisions to the employees or candidates those decisions affected. The tool was deployed, produces outputs, and the decision-making process inside the tool is opaque to the organization that deployed it. This is the specific organizational condition that makes AI bias claims legally actionable — not just biased outputs, but the inability to demonstrate what the algorithm did and whether the organization took steps to assess bias.
Financial Consequence
NYC LL144 fines are currently modest but the class action exposure for organizations without bias audits on AI hiring tools is significant and growing. The EEOC has signaled it will treat AI bias as equivalent to intentional discrimination when the organization can't demonstrate bias assessment steps. This is the condition whose financial consequence narrative is growing fastest in the taxonomy — the regulatory and litigation trajectory is clear and accelerating.

Wage Theft and Off-the-Clock Work
Annual Scale
$50B+ (EPI estimate)
vs. Property Crime
More than all combined
Mechanism
Cultural pressure, not instruction
What the Litigation Reveals
Off-the-clock work claims cluster in organizations with a specific cultural dynamic: the operational expectation has expanded beyond the paid workday without the organization acknowledging it. Managers don't explicitly require off-the-clock work. They reward employees who do it and implicitly penalize employees who don't. The cultural expectation is the mechanism. Individual manager behavior is the delivery system. The organization has a compliant timekeeping policy. The culture ignores it.
Organizational Condition Underneath
Cultural Overtime — operational expectations produce compensable work outside paid hours through cultural pressure rather than explicit instruction. The organization has a compliant policy. The culture ignores it. Managers don't instruct employees to work off the clock. They create the conditions where not working off the clock produces visible performance consequences. The policy is legal. The culture is not. Distinct from Structural Overload — that's too much work. Cultural Overtime is too much expected availability.
Financial Consequence
Economic Policy Institute estimates $50B+ in annual wage theft — more than all property crime combined. Class action wage theft litigation in California and New York produces settlements in the tens of millions for large employers with systematic Cultural Overtime. The multiplier — back wages plus liquidated damages plus attorney fees — produces 2x the underlying back wages as the effective organizational cost under federal law; some states separately permit treble damages. The condition is highly prevalent; the discovery mechanism (class action attorneys) is highly efficient at finding it.
Conditions Surfaced

Key Findings for PRV3
This experiment produces the financial consequence narratives directly
Every other experiment required inference to reach financial consequence — this behavior produces this outcome, which costs approximately this much based on research. Litigation data skips the inference. The EEOC recovered $665 million. The DOL recovered $259 million in FY2025 — though the more significant development is procedural: the agency stopped seeking liquidated damages in administrative settlements, reducing the deterrent value of voluntary compliance. The SEC has awarded $1.9 billion. These are the numbers the Patterned Consequence Logic (the engine framework linking organizational conditions to quantified financial outcomes) uses. The 109 Hammer Index citations are the source library. This experiment identified which conditions each citation belongs to.
Internal Reporting Failure has the most extreme consequence narrative
The gap between the cost of fixing the internal reporting mechanism and the cost of external regulatory exposure is orders of magnitude. This condition produces the largest uncapped financial exposure in the taxonomy. It should be treated as a high-priority state with a financial consequence narrative that makes the cost-of-inaction case more powerfully than any other condition. The SEC's largest single award was $279 million — paid because the internal mechanism failed to work.
Sequential Decision Blindness is the most structurally invisible condition
No individual in the organization intends to retaliate. The sequence constitutes retaliation. The organization pays for intent it didn't have. This condition is invisible to every standard diagnostic tool — no survey, no exit interview, no culture assessment surfaces it. The PRV3 diagnostic is uniquely positioned to detect Sequential Decision Blindness because the instrument looks at the governance architecture rather than individual behavior. This is the clearest case where the diagnostic produces value that no other tool can.
Twelve existing conditions confirmed by litigation data
The Unformed Leader, The Untouchable, The Exposed, HR Capture, Misconduct Infrastructure Absence, Organizational Deafness, Hiring Bias and Selection Inconsistency, Unmanaged Underperformance, Regulatory Exposure, Culture Driven Burnout, AI Governance Failure, and Structural Overload are all confirmed by litigation data. Every confirmation adds a specific financial consequence value to the existing condition profile — the litigation data is the most authoritative financial evidence available.
