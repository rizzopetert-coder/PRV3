### change-management-failure-rate

**1. CURRENT FINDING**

This claim has a complex and well-documented scholarly history that PRV3 should treat with precision. The audit located the following layered picture:

**Origin of the figure:**

The earliest traceable version appears in Michael Hammer and James Champy's 1993 book *Reengineering the Corporation*, in which they wrote that "our unscientific estimate is that as many as 50 per cent to 70 per cent of the organizations that undertake a reengineering effort do not achieve the dramatic results they intended."
 This was explicitly self-described as an unscientific estimate, scoped specifically to *reengineering efforts*, not to change management in general.

**How the figure migrated and became "70%":**

Beer and Nohria's 2000 *HBR* article "Cracking the Code of Change" opens with the line "The brutal fact is that about 70% of all change initiatives fail" — with no footnote or source cited.
 
McKinsey's 2009 article "The Inconvenient Truth about Change Management" (Keller and Aiken) surveyed 1,546 business executives and found that only about 30% considered their change programs "completely or mostly successful" — but this was a self-reported executive survey, not a controlled study of program outcomes.
 McKinsey's own subsequent 2021 report, *Losing from Day One*, cited Kotter as the source in a footnote — completing a circular citation chain in which McKinsey cites Kotter, Kotter's figure derives from observation rather than measurement, and Beer & Nohria's assertion carries no citation at all.

**Primary academic verdict:**

Mark Hughes (2011), in a peer-reviewed paper in the *Journal of Change Management* (Vol. 11, No. 4, "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?"), examined five separate published instances claiming a 70% failure rate — Hammer and Champy, Beer and Nohria, a Bain article, a McKinsey article, and Kotter — and concluded: "Whilst the existence of a popular narrative of 70 per cent organizational change failure is acknowledged, there is no valid and reliable empirical evidence to support such a narrative."
 In each instance Hughes found the same pattern: 
each source either stated the number without evidence or cited another source that stated it without evidence — "The citations are like nesting dolls: each one opens to reveal another citation inside, never to lead us anywhere interesting."


**Current state of the literature (2024–2025):**
The Hughes (2011) finding has not been superseded or contested by new primary empirical data. 
A 2024–2025 aggregated industry research summary indicates that only approximately 32% of change initiatives are clearly successful — meaning roughly two-thirds either fail or underperform
 — but this figure comes from a practitioner compilation, not a peer-reviewed study with defined failure criteria. 
A 2021 Errida & Lotfi study is cited by some sources as reporting "60–70% of change initiatives fail," while a CEB Corporate Leadership Council (2016) study found 50% outright failures, 16% mixed results, and only 34% successes
 — but these figures use varying definitions of "failure" and varying study populations. 
Recent industry reports (Bain 2024, Mutaree 2024) report even higher failure figures (88% and 77% respectively)
, though these are consultant-produced surveys subject to the same methodological critiques as the original McKinsey figures.

**The disciplinary consensus as of 2025:**
The "70%" figure as a *precise, sourced, empirically validated* claim is academically indefensible. What the literature actually supports is: (a) a large fraction of change programs fail to meet their stated objectives; (b) the precise fraction is highly sensitive to how "failure" is defined and who is doing the measuring; (c) the 70% figure specifically traces to an admitted self-estimate in a 1993 reengineering book and has been laundered into apparent authority through circular citation. The figure is best characterized as a *popularized heuristic*, not a research finding.

**Sources:**
- Hughes, M. (2011). "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?" *Journal of Change Management*, 11(4), 451–464. https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14697017.2011.630506
- University of Brighton research summary: https://research.brighton.ac.uk/en/publications/do-70-per-cent-of-all-organizational-change-initiatives-really-fa/
- StrategyU analysis with citation chain reconstruction: https://strategyu.co/do-70-percent-of-change-initiatives-fail/

The $295M appears to be a third-party transcription error and should not be used. The authoritative figure is **$259M**.

**Mechanism change — liquidated damages:**

Pursuant to DOL guidance FAB 2025-3, issued on June 27, 2025, the WHD is no longer permitted to seek liquidated damages in any pre-litigation investigation or resolution unless a private lawsuit is filed by an employee or the secretary of labor.
 
Effective June 27, 2025, the DOL will only supervise or require the payment of unpaid wages or overtime compensation during administrative investigations and settlements; DOL staff and legal counsel are now prohibited from seeking, negotiating
 or collecting liquidated damages in pre-litigation settlement of wage and hour disputes. 
The new guidance reinforces that only courts may award liquidated damages and clarifies the WHD's role in pre-litigation investigations and resolutions.


The effective date is **June 27, 2025** (FAB 2025-3), not a range of "June/July 2025." The last-verified entry's "June/July 2025" bracket is correct as a conservative range but the specific instrument date is June 27, 2025.

**Rousseau (psychological contract) — important disambiguation required.**
A cluster of recent retractions in *Group & Organization Management* (Griep, Kraak, et al., 2024–2026) do name Rousseau as a *co-author* on applied impact papers, not on her foundational psychological contract work. 
The retracted 2024 paper "In Pursuit of Impact: How Psychological Contract Research Can Make the Work-World a Better Place" (Kraak, Hansen, Griep, … Rousseau et al.) was retracted by the journal
 due to data issues in that specific study. This is emphatically **not** a retraction of Rousseau's foundational work (e.g., 1989, 1995). The distinction is critical for PRV3 purposes: Rousseau's name appearing on a retracted applied paper does not impugn the underlying psychological contract construct. Flag for awareness; no action required on the foundational citation.

**Keltner (HC-103) — notable development.**
No retraction of Keltner's underlying power-cognition research was found. However, a significant 2025 meta-analytic synthesis directly tested and partially recontextualized Keltner et al.'s (2003) approach-inhibition theory of power — the theoretical backbone of HC-103. 
Sabey, Shanklin, Colquitt, and Baer (2025), published in *Psychological Bulletin* (Vol. 151, No. 10, pp. 1245–1279, DOI: 10.1037/bul0000500), conducted the first large-scale meta-analytic test of the approach-inhibition theory, noting that "the past 20 years of research have surfaced several potential issues with the theory, including empirical dissensus, mediational ambiguity, and questions about cognitive versus affective mechanisms."
 
Their overall conclusion, however, was that "the theory is well-supported; approach associations are larger than inhibition associations; power influences behavior indirectly through attention, cognition, and affect; and those indirect effects are largely conveyed through affect."
 This is a significant new meta-analytic confirmation, not a falsification — but it adds nuance around *how* the mechanism operates (primarily through affect, not purely through impaired social cognition as popular accounts often frame it).
