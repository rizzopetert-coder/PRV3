#### NYC Local Law 144


The New York State Comptroller's December 2025 audit evaluated DCWP's enforcement of Local Law 144, which regulates the use of automated employment decision tools (AEDTs) in hiring and promotion. The audit concluded that current enforcement of the law is "ineffective," citing, among other things, problematic complaint intake.


Specifically: 
For its first two years, Local Law 144 was a law with real requirements and, in practice, thin enforcement. That changed with a New York State Comptroller audit published December 2, 2025, which reviewed DCWP's enforcement of the law from July 2023 through June 2025 and found it, in the Comptroller's own language, "ineffective."
 
The specifics are unflattering: DCWP surveyed the websites and bias audits of 32 companies and identified a single instance of non-compliance. Comptroller auditors reviewed the same 32 companies using the enforcement procedures DCWP was supposed to follow and found a significantly higher non-compliance rate.



With DCWP committing to adopt most of the audit's recommendations, employers using AEDTs should prepare for stronger, more structured enforcement of Local Law 144 going forward.
 
Employment counsel (e.g., DLA Piper, January 2026) are warning employers to expect a new phase of stricter enforcement.


PRV3 audit note: The underlying law and its core annual-bias-audit requirement remain unchanged and in force. The material development is the Comptroller's formal "ineffective" finding and DCWP's commitment to reform its enforcement process — a significant enforcement-posture shift, not a legislative change to the law itself.

#### EU AI Act — Employment Provisions

The EU AI Act is subject to a significant deadline change materially affecting the employment-specific (Annex III) high-risk obligations:


The European Commission published the Digital Omnibus on AI on 19 November 2025, proposing to defer the high-risk compliance deadline from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027.



On May 7, 2026, the Council of the EU and the European Parliament announced their provisional agreement on targeted amendments to the EU AI Act, as part of the European Commission's Digital Omnibus initiative launched in late 2025, which proposed to delay the high-risk compliance deadline.
 
On June 29, 2026, the Council of the European Union gave final approval to the "Digital Omnibus" simplification package, formally pushing back the compliance deadline for high-risk AI systems under Annex III of the EU AI Act from August 2, 2026, to December 2, 2027 — a 16-month delay.



If your organisation uses AI systems covered by Annex III (standalone high-risk AI such as recruitment AI, credit scoring, or biometric categorisation), the deadline is now 2 December 2027.



2 August 2026 remains an active compliance date. The Article 50 transparency obligations for AI systems largely remain on the original schedule.
 Prohibited practices (including emotion recognition in workplace contexts) have been in force since February 2, 2025, and that has not changed.


The main date for staffing businesses is now 2 December 2027, when the full suite of high-risk system obligations becomes enforceable for Annex III systems, including all employment-related AI.

### change-management-failure-rate

**1. CURRENT FINDING**

This claim has now been independently verified against primary sources. The conclusion is unambiguous: **the "70% of change programs fail" figure is an unsourced assertion that has propagated through circular citation with no empirical foundation.**

**Tracing the primary source chain:**

The earliest traceable instance is Hammer & Champy (1993, *Reengineering the Corporation*), which stated: 
"Our unscientific estimate is that as many as 50 percent to 70 percent of the organizations that undertake a reengineering effort do not achieve the dramatic results they intended."
 The authors explicitly called it unscientific, and Hammer walked the figure back in 1995.

The most-cited source for the "70%" claim is Beer & Nohria's (2000) HBR article *"Cracking the Code of Change."* 
The version most people cite comes from Michael Beer and Nitin Nohria's 2000 HBR article "Cracking the Code of Change." Their opening line: "The brutal fact is that about 70% of all change initiatives fail." No footnote or source for this claim.


The definitive academic deconstruction was published by Mark Hughes (University of Brighton) in the *Journal of Change Management* (2011). 
In a 2011 paper in the Journal of Change Management, he examined the five most prominent published sources for the 70% claim: Hammer and Champy, Beer and Nohria, a Bain & Company article, a McKinsey article, and Kotter.
 His conclusion: 
"Whilst the existence of a popular narrative of 70 per cent organizational change failure is acknowledged, there is no valid and reliable empirical evidence to support such a narrative."
 
In every case, Hughes found the same pattern. Each source either stated the number without evidence, or cited another source that stated it without evidence.


**What McKinsey's actual data show:** The figure that most consulting attributions point to as "McKinsey research" is not a measured failure rate. 
In 2008, the Organizational Behavioral Practice arm of McKinsey published "The Inconvenient Truth About Change Management." One of their findings was that only 30% agreed that their change programs were "completely/mostly successful."
 This is a self-reported executive satisfaction survey — from which "70% fail" is an inversion — not a controlled measurement of program outcomes. Furthermore, this McKinsey article itself 
incorrectly referenced John Kotter's 1995 article, then shared the results of their own survey.


**Current standing (2025–2026):** The academic consensus has not changed since Hughes (2011) — the figure remains mythologized. 
No one has cleanly measured a 70 percent digital transformation failure rate. The figure fuses McKinsey's broad change-failure number with the authority of its separate digital survey, and echoes a 1993 estimate the authors called unscientific.
 As recently as August 2025, the Agile Change Leadership Institute confirmed that no peer-reviewed research has validated the statistic. The StrategyU analysis (March 2026) describes the citation chain as "nesting dolls: each one opens to reveal another citation inside, never to lead us anywhere interesting."

**Distortion flag (per PRV3 audit rigor):** Some 2024–2025 consulting and industry sources continue to assert the 70% figure (e.g., Bain reportedly claiming 88% in 2024; some BCG/McKinsey digital-specific surveys citing 70–73%). These are not primary research — they are practitioner surveys with undefined success criteria, recycled in consulting marketing materials. They do not validate the underlying statistic.
