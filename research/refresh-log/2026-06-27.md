### change-management-failure-rate

**1. CURRENT FINDING**

The "70% of change programs fail" figure has been extensively investigated and the picture is now well-established in peer-reviewed literature:

**Origin tracing:** The claim does not originate from a single primary study. 
In their widely cited article, Beer and Nohria state that approximately 70% of change initiatives fail to achieve their objectives — but other researchers, such as Hughes, argue that there is insufficient empirical evidence to support this claim.
 The specific Beer & Nohria source is their 2000 *HBR* article "Cracking the Code of Change," which 
states, with no reference or evidence whatsoever, "The brutal fact is that about 70% of all change initiatives fail."


**The Hughes (2011) verdict:** 
The University of Brighton's Mark Hughes found, in a 2011 *Journal of Change Management* peer-reviewed study, that there is no empirical evidence to support the 70% failure statistic.
 The University of Brighton's institutional repository confirms the conclusion directly: 
"Whilst the existence of a popular narrative of 70 percent organizational change failure is acknowledged, there is no valid and reliable empirical evidence to support such a narrative."
 Hughes critically reviewed five separate published instances of the 70% claim and found the same absence of underlying data in each.

**What the chain of sources actually looks like:** 
The figure originates from early observations by consultants like Hammer & Champy in the 1990s during business process reengineering, and was later popularized by Beer & Nohria without offering detailed research evidence — these figures were then echoed in consulting presentations and business articles.
 Kotter's contribution is similarly un-sourced: in 2008, Kotter wrote an estimate based on "years of study" rather than a primary data study. 
The LinkedIn analysis of the claim's lineage notes that Beer and Nohria's 2000 statement had no supporting evidence in the article itself.


**The McKinsey attribution specifically:** Multiple sources attribute the 70% figure to "McKinsey" (including a 2019 *PubMed*-indexed article). However, no original McKinsey primary research publication establishing this as an empirical finding has been independently located. The McKinsey attribution appears to be a secondary-circulation artifact — the figure circulates *through* consulting ecosystem publications, not *from* a citable McKinsey primary study. This is a classic distorted headline figure of the type PRV3 flags: a popularized estimate that has shed its non-empirical origins in transit.

**Current best available data (2024–2025):** More recent practitioner-sourced data offers a rough empirical grounding, though with significant methodological caveats. 
A 2024–2025 research synthesis indicates only approximately 32% of change initiatives are clearly successful — meaning roughly two-thirds either fail or underperform.

only 43% of employees believe their organization manages change effectively, down from 60% in 2019 (WTW, 2023).
 These figures are directionally consistent with the "most change programs struggle" thesis, but they are practitioner surveys, not controlled studies — and they measure different constructs than "failure rate."

### dol-back-wages-and-mechanism

### dol-back-wages-and-mechanism

**1. CURRENT FINDING**

**Back-wages figure:** 
In fiscal year 2025, WHD's enforcement efforts resulted in more than $259 million in back wages for nearly 176,957 employees, an average of $1,465 per worker — the highest recovery since 2019.
 This is confirmed by the primary DOL press release (https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/whd/whd20260108, January 8, 2026) and the WHD data page (https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/data).

**PRV3 Distortion Check — $295M Secondary-Source Discrepancy:** One secondary source (Deeley Insurance Group, January 16, 2026) cites "$295 million in back wages" for FY2025. This figure does not appear in DOL's own press release, WHD data page, or any other corroborated source. The most probable explanation is that the $295M figure conflates back wages with liquidated damages under the pre-June 2025 methodology — i.e., it may represent the gross sum workers would have recovered under the old regime (unpaid wages + equal liquidated damages), rather than the official back-wages-only figure. The authoritative DOL primary source figure is unambiguously **$259 million**. The $295M figure should be treated as a secondary-source distortion and disregarded.

**Mechanism — liquidated damages elimination:** 
On June 27, 2025, the DOL announced a significant shift in its wage and hour enforcement policy, ending the longstanding practice of seeking liquidated damages in administrative, pre-litigation proceedings under the Fair Labor Standards Act, outlined in Field Assistance Bulletin (FAB) No. 2025-3. DOL staff and legal counsel are now prohibited from seeking, negotiating for, or approving the inclusion of liquidated damages in any administrative (pre-litigation) settlement.



The only exception to this rule is for matters where parties had already reached a prior agreement.



The revised enforcement position applies broadly to all pre-litigation activity under Section 216(c). WHD may no longer request, encourage, negotiate, accept, or approve the payment of liquidated damages at any point prior to filing a lawsuit.
 
Liquidated damages still apply in court cases, so employers remain at risk in litigation.


**Primary Sources:**
- DOL WHD Press Release: https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/whd/whd20260108 (January 8, 2026)
- DOL WHD Data Page: https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/data
- FAB No. 2025-3 coverage: https://www.laboremploymentlawblog.com/2025/07/articles/department-of-labor/department-of-labor-curtails-liquidated-damages-in-wage-and-hour-investigations/ (July 23, 2025)
- National Law Review: https://natlawreview.com/article/ding-dong-us-dol-assessment-liquidated-damages-dead (June 30, 2025)

This is not a retraction; it is a first formal meta-analytic synthesis that both confirms the theory's core and introduces important structural nuance.
