### change-management-failure-rate

**1. CURRENT FINDING:** This is the first substantive verification of this figure (previously only flagged as pending). Tracing the "70% of change programs fail" claim to its primary sources:

- The earliest traceable version is **Hammer & Champy's 1993** *Reengineering the Corporation*, which the authors themselves explicitly labeled as a guess: 
"Our unscientific estimate is that as many as 50 per cent to 70 per cent of the organizations that undertake a reengineering effort do not achieve the dramatic results they intended."

- McKinsey's own commonly-cited version comes from its 2015 article "Changing change management," which asserts 
"Change management as it is traditionally applied is outdated. We know, for example, that 70 percent of change programs fail to achieve their goals, largely due to employee resistance and lack of management support."
 Independent tracing found 
McKinsey's 2015 article states that 70 percent of change programs fail to achieve their goals, with no source note, method, or sample shown behind it
, and a later 2020 McKinsey piece and 2021 report recycle the figure via a Forbes column and a reference back to Kotter rather than new primary data.
- The key academic rebuttal is **Mark Hughes (2011)**, *Journal of Change Management* 11(4), "Do 70 Per Cent of All Organizational Change Initiatives Really Fail?" 
"This paper critically reviews five separate published instances identifying a 70 percent organizational change failure rate."
 The study's conclusion, cited elsewhere, found 
"a 2011 study in the Journal of Change Management, led by the University of Brighton researcher Mark Hughes found that there is no empirical evidence to support this statistic."

- A 2022 follow-up in the same journal reiterates that the underlying data have never materialized: 
"I have unsuccessfully searched for the evidence to substantiate change and transformation failure tendency claims in general, and in particular claims that 70% of all change initiatives fail (Hughes, 2011). Almost a decade later, failure rhetoric remains academically fashionable."

- A more recent independent tracing exercise (2025) concludes bluntly: 
"We found no credible study that establishes 70 percent as a measured digital transformation failure rate under a consistent definition."

- Sources checked: strategyu.co (dated ~193 days ago), reliamag.com (dated ~94 days ago), jdsupra.com, tandfonline.com (2022 Journal of Change Management editorial), researchgate.net/University of Brighton repository for Hughes (2011). Dates as noted above; check performed in current session.

**2. CHANGE ASSESSMENT:** Material change — not because the number itself has moved, but because the epistemic status of the figure is materially different from how a "70% fail" statistic is typically presented in business writing (including, per the citing excerpt, consulting-postmortem content). Primary-source tracing shows the figure originated as an explicitly **unscientific, self-labeled estimate** about reengineering projects specifically (not all "change programs"), was never empirically re-derived by McKinsey or anyone else, and has been directly refuted by peer-reviewed research (Hughes 2011; reaffirmed 2022) finding no supporting evidence. This is a classic "distorted headline figure" case per PRV3 audit rigor: a vague, unsourced guess from one 1993 book was laundered into an authoritative-sounding "research shows" statistic repeated by McKinsey, Kotter-attributed claims, and countless secondary sources.

**4.** No single revised numeric figure exists to replace "70%" — the academic record does not offer a validated alternative failure rate; it instead documents the absence of empirical grounding for any fixed percentage. This itself should be treated as the finding, not a gap to fill with a different number.
