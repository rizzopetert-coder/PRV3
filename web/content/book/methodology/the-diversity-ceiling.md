# The Diversity Ceiling

Look up from wherever you currently sit in the organization, and the people occupying the levels above you gradually stop resembling the people actually around you day to day. Not a smooth, gradual thinning as you move up the org chart. A genuinely clear, identifiable line, above which the overall composition of the organization visibly changes.

Leadership can reasonably point to solid representation numbers at the entry level and feel genuinely good about that specific achievement. Those same numbers consistently don't survive the actual climb upward through the organization, and everyone currently sitting below the ceiling already has a fairly accurate sense of roughly where that ceiling sits, even without anyone officially naming it.

---

### Entry-Level Numbers Measure the Wrong Stage

An organization can genuinely have strong, defensible representation numbers at the point of initial hiring and still have a real ceiling above which that same representation consistently collapses. Those are two genuinely different measurements, and typically only one of them is being closely tracked and reported on with any real rigor. Reporting the entry-level number confidently as evidence of overall organizational progress, while the considerably less favorable senior-level number quietly tells a very different and more complicated story, isn't necessarily a deliberately dishonest choice on leadership's part. It's usually simply measuring the specific stage that happens to be easiest to measure and easiest to feel genuinely good about publicly, while the stage that actually determines meaningful long-term outcomes for people goes largely unexamined by comparison.

### Why the Gap Is Invisible From the Top and Obvious From Below

From a vantage point above the ceiling, this particular pattern is genuinely difficult to see clearly. Leadership's own personal path upward through the organization typically never required them to cross that specific barrier, so it simply isn't part of their own direct lived experience of the organization. From below the ceiling, by clear contrast, the pattern is specific, concrete, and readily nameable: people positioned below the ceiling can usually describe, often in considerable detail, precisely which promotion decisions, which sponsorship patterns, and which informal unstated criteria actually produced the gap they're personally watching unfold. This asymmetry in who can actually perceive the pattern clearly is a meaningful part of why it persists over time so reliably — the specific people who hold the actual power to fix it are, structurally, the people least likely to have personally experienced it themselves.

### Where the Composition Actually Shifts

**Representation is meaningfully and measurably stronger at entry and mid-levels of the organization than it is at senior and executive levels, with a genuinely visible drop-off occurring at a specific, identifiable point.** Not a smooth, gradual taper as you'd naturally expect. An identifiable specific level where the overall composition clearly and noticeably shifts.

**People positioned below the ceiling can describe specific, concrete instances where advancement clearly diverged from actual output.** Ask people privately, and the specific examples come readily and easily — this particular pattern is genuinely not abstract to the people actually living through it day to day.

**Sponsorship relationships and stretch-assignment opportunities concentrate noticeably among people who already closely resemble current leadership.** The genuine informal pipeline that actually ends up producing senior leaders is running, in practice, on a considerably narrower and more homogeneous set of relationships than the formal promotion process on paper would ever suggest.

### Two Different Problems, One Budget Line

Continued and genuine investment in strengthening entry-level representation, while entirely real and absolutely worth sustaining in its own right, doesn't meaningfully address the underlying mechanism that's actually producing the ceiling above it — those are genuinely different problems that require genuinely different kinds of intervention, and continuing to invest further in the one that's already demonstrably working doesn't meaningfully touch the one that clearly isn't.

### Moving the Ceiling Requires Tracking the Climb, Not the Entry

**First, measure representation carefully at every single level of the organization, not merely at the point of initial hiring — and specifically track the exact point where the meaningful drop-off actually occurs.** You genuinely can't fix a gap you haven't first located with real precision.

**Second, audit sponsorship relationships and stretch-assignment patterns specifically at the levels positioned just below the ceiling, which is where the real informal pipeline to senior roles actually forms in practice.** This is usually where the underlying mechanism genuinely lives, considerably more than it lives in the formal, documented promotion criteria themselves.

**Third, set and actively track a specific numerical target for closing the gap at precisely the level where it's currently worst — not simply a single organization-wide average that conveniently allows a strong entry-level number to offset a genuinely weak senior-level one.** An aggregate average effectively hides exactly the specific pattern this piece is describing.

The entry-level number was never actually the genuinely hard part of this problem to solve. The climb upward is, and it's consistently the part that's gone the most unmeasured.
