You have a list. Somewhere — maybe in a shared drive, maybe in an HR system, maybe in a doc that gets dusted off once a year for the board meeting — there's a list of names that are supposed to be your succession plan.

The CEO has two names next to her role. The CFO has one. The VP of Engineering has a question mark because the obvious internal candidate left eight months ago and you haven't updated it. A few critical roles have "external hire" written next to them, which is another way of saying "we don't have anyone."

That's not a succession plan. That's a wish.

A plan would answer the question: if this person left tomorrow — not "announced a transition" but left, tomorrow, no warning — what specifically happens next? Who steps in? Are they ready? What support do they need? What does the first ninety days look like?

If you can't answer those questions with specifics, you don't have a plan. You have hope. And hope is not a strategy for organizational continuity.

## Why This Feels Less Urgent Than It Is

Succession planning is one of those things that's obviously important and perpetually deprioritized.

There's always something more immediate. The quarter has to close. The product has to ship. The reorg has to land. Succession planning is a long-term investment in a future state that might not happen, competing against short-term fires that are definitely happening right now.

And there's something psychologically weird about succession planning. It requires imagining your own absence, or imagining the absence of people you depend on. That's uncomfortable. It's easier to assume continuity than to plan for disruption.

So the list gets made once. It goes in a folder. Once a year, maybe, someone asks about it. The names get shuffled around a little. Everyone feels responsible without anyone actually being accountable for whether the plan is real.

Then someone important leaves, gets sick, gets recruited, burns out, retires, dies (it happens), and suddenly the thing that was never urgent is the only thing that matters.

## The Leverage Problem

Here's the part that connects succession planning to organizational friction.

When there's no credible succession plan for a critical role, the person in that role has leverage. Maybe they don't use it. Maybe they're not even aware of it. But the organization has created a structural dependency on a single person, and that dependency shapes every interaction.

The person can't be challenged as directly because the cost of them leaving is too high. Feedback gets softened. Accountability gets deferred. Behaviors that would be addressed in anyone else get tolerated because "we can't afford to lose them."

This is The Untouchable territory. And it's often preventable. The leverage that makes someone untouchable usually accrues over time, as the organization fails to develop alternatives. If you'd built the bench three years ago, you wouldn't be in this position now.

Succession planning isn't just about continuity. It's about maintaining organizational flexibility. It's about not being held hostage by any single person, no matter how talented. It's about preserving your ability to have honest conversations and enforce standards evenly.

## Critical Roles vs. Critical People

One of the mental traps in succession planning is confusing the criticality of a role with the irreplaceability of a person.

Yes, the VP of Engineering is critical. The organization cannot function without that role being filled by someone competent. That's a structural reality.

But is *this specific VP of Engineering* irreplaceable? That's a different question. And the answer is almost always no. They're great at their job — that's why they're in it — but there are other people on the planet who could do this job. Maybe not identically. Maybe not without transition costs. But the role can be filled.

The succession plan exists to make that transition manageable. To ensure that when the current person leaves — and they will leave, eventually, one way or another — the organization doesn't fall into chaos.

The people who are most resistant to succession planning are often the people who benefit from being seen as irreplaceable. That's not always conscious. But the conversation about who could succeed someone is also, implicitly, a conversation about whether that person is as uniquely essential as they've been treated.

## What a Real Plan Looks Like

A wish list has names. A plan has answers.

**Who's ready now?** Is there anyone who could step into this role tomorrow with minimal ramp? If not, what's the gap, and what would it take to close it?

**Who's ready in 12-18 months?** Who's on a development trajectory toward this role? What experiences do they need between now and then? Is someone actively managing that development, or is it just assumed to be happening?

**What's the external market?** If you had to go outside, what would that look like? What's the timeline? What are the risks of external hires in this specific role? Have you maintained the relationships that would let you move quickly?

**What happens in the first 90 days?** If the transition happens tomorrow, what does the interim state look like? Who makes decisions while you're searching? What authority do they have? How do you prevent the organization from stalling?

If you can answer these questions for your critical roles, you have something like a plan. If you can't, you have a list.

## The Conversation Nobody Wants to Have

Sometimes the succession planning conversation surfaces something uncomfortable.

Like the fact that there's no obvious successor because the current leader has been a bottleneck for development. The people who might have grown into the role left because there was no path, or were never given the opportunities that would have prepared them.

Like the fact that the "critical" person isn't actually performing at the level their criticality would suggest. They're protected not because they're excellent but because the organization has made itself dependent on them and is afraid of what happens if they go.

Like the fact that the succession question is really a question about whether this role should exist in its current form at all, or whether a reorganization would actually solve more problems than a replacement.

Succession planning, done honestly, often leads to other conversations. That's not a bug. The questions it surfaces are questions the organization probably needs to answer anyway. Better to answer them now, on your timeline, than later, on someone else's.

## The Question We Ask

When we work with an organization on succession planning, we don't start with the org chart. We start with a question.

*If your most critical person left tomorrow, what specifically would you do on day one?*

The specificity matters. Not "we'd figure it out": what would you actually do? Who would you call? What decisions would need to be made? What would you tell the team?

If the answer is clear and actionable, you're in better shape than most. If the answer is vague or panicked, that's information about where the work needs to happen.

Succession planning isn't about predicting the future. It's about being prepared for transitions that are guaranteed to happen eventually. The only question is whether they happen on your terms or someone else's.
