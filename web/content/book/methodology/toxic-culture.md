Toxic cultures rarely announce themselves with an incident. They teach the rules by example — who gets protected, who gets pushed out, what happens to the person who said the thing everyone else was already thinking — until everyone in the building has learned which parts of themselves to leave in the car before walking in.

The values are usually on the wall somewhere, printed in the right font. The gap between what's written there and what actually gets rewarded is where the culture actually lives, and most people working inside it can describe that gap in specific, granular detail well before anyone with the authority to close it admits it exists.

---

## How It Gets This Way

Nobody sets out to build a toxic culture. That's the thing. It's not a plan. It's an accumulation.

It starts with one pass. One behavior that should have been addressed and wasn't. A leader who runs hot, but hey, they deliver results. A founding team dynamic that's "just how they are." A hire who's kind of a nightmare but knows the codebase better than anyone, so everyone learns to work around them. You tell yourself it's temporary. You tell yourself it's contained. And then five years later you're explaining to a new hire that "yeah, that's just how it is here" like that's a normal thing to say about a workplace.

Each pass sets a precedent. The organization learns what it will tolerate. The people inside it learn what's actually rewarded versus what's written in the handbook. The gap between those two things is where the rot lives.

The ones who can't tolerate it leave. Usually quietly. Usually with a two-week notice and an exit interview where they say something polite about "pursuing other opportunities." HR checks a box. The departure gets filed under "voluntary attrition." Nobody asks why your third senior engineer in eighteen months just left for a ten percent raise she could have gotten here if anyone had bothered to ask what was wrong.

The ones who stay either adapt or go internal. They learn to keep their head down. They learn the rules: the real ones, not the ones on the poster. They stop expecting things to change. And slowly, without anyone deciding it should happen, the culture becomes a machine that selects for people who can survive it and ejects people who can't.

That's not a strategy. It's just what happens when nobody's willing to name the thing.

## The Three Tells

You can diagnose a toxic culture without a survey. You just have to know where to look.

**Who's leaving.** Not the number — the pattern. Are you losing your best people at the eighteen-month mark? Are they all from the same team, or the same level, or the same profile? Do they leave and then quietly warn their friends not to apply? The exit interview won't tell you this. The exit interview is theater. What they say to their friends six months later: that's the real data.

**Who's silent.** In a healthy culture, people push back. They ask hard questions. They say "I don't think that's going to work" in the meeting instead of in the parking lot afterward. In a toxic culture, the silence is loud. Nobody challenges the plan. Nobody flags the risk. Everyone nods, and then the thing fails exactly the way three people in the room knew it would fail, and nobody's surprised except the person who should have been told.

**Who's thriving.** This is the uncomfortable one. Look at who's getting promoted. Look at who's protected. Look at who gets described as "high-performing" or "a key player." Now look at how they treat people. If there's a gap between how they behave and what the values say, that gap is the culture. The rest is marketing.

## Why It's So Hard to Fix

Here's the thing nobody wants to say out loud: toxic cultures are stable.

Not healthy. Not productive. Not good for anyone long-term. But stable. The people who couldn't handle it are gone. The people who remain have learned the rules. The behaviors that created the toxicity are now load-bearing: they're attached to power, to tenure, to relationships that feel too important to disrupt. You'd have to blow something up to change it, and nobody wants to be the one holding the match.

So instead, you get initiatives. A new values rollout. A culture committee. A leadership offsite where everyone talks about "how we want to show up" and then comes back Monday and shows up exactly the same way they always have. It's not that people are lying. It's that the system is stronger than the intention.

The CEO announces that things are going to be different. Everyone nods. The person who's the actual source of the toxicity sits in the front row, nodding hardest. Nothing changes. The people who've been waiting for change do the math one more time and decide this is the year they finally update their resume.

You've seen this movie. You know how it ends.

---

## What Actually Works

Fixing a toxic culture requires doing the thing the culture was designed to avoid: naming it and acting on what you named.

That's it. That's the whole thing. Everything else is decoration.

It means looking directly at the behaviors that are being protected and deciding you're done protecting them. It means having the conversation with the person everyone's been working around, the real conversation, not the feedback-sandwiched version that lets everyone feel like they tried. It means being willing to lose the person who "delivers results" if the results come with a body count.

The first time a protected behavior meets an actual consequence, the culture shifts. The shift has less to do with punishment than with visibility: everyone else in the organization sees that the rules are real now. That the gap between the poster and the practice just got a lot smaller.

That's the moment people start to believe things might actually change. And that belief — that "oh, they're serious" moment — is where culture change actually starts.

Everything before that is just the values poster with better lighting.

## The Question We Ask

When someone tells us they have a culture problem, we don't start with a survey. We start with a question.

*Who in this organization is currently protected from consequences that would apply to anyone else, and what would it cost to stop protecting them?*

Sometimes the answer is quick. They've known for years. They've just been waiting for someone to say it out loud in a room where it might actually lead somewhere.

Sometimes the answer is complicated. The protected person is a founder, or a top seller, or someone's college roommate. The cost isn't just a conversation — it's a reckoning with how the organization got here and who benefits from it staying this way.

Either way, that's where the work starts. Not with the values poster. Not with the engagement survey. With the specific, named, human-shaped problem that everyone knows about and nobody has been willing to touch.

We've been in that room before. We know what it takes to have that conversation and what happens after you have it.

Naming the specific, human-shaped problem everyone already knows about is the only part of this that actually works.
