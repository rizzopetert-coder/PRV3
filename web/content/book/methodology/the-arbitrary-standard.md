# The Arbitrary Standard

The rules don't apply the same way to everyone here, and people have noticed this over time, in specific and recountable instances rather than as a vague general impression. Someone got away with something that someone else, in a genuinely comparable situation, was formally disciplined for — and it wasn't a single, isolated inconsistency easily explained away. It has a real, discernible pattern, and people have quietly decoded that pattern for themselves.

---

### Inconsistency Reads as Information, Not Noise

A single inconsistent decision, taken entirely on its own, might genuinely be a one-off — a manager's individual misjudgment in a difficult moment, a genuinely unique circumstance that doesn't generalize to anything broader. What actually makes this a durable condition rather than a passing incident is repetition that forms a recognizable pattern over time. People are, generally speaking, quite good pattern-matchers when it comes to questions of fairness specifically, and once they've correctly identified who the rules seem to consistently bend for, that read becomes their operative working belief about how the organization genuinely functions — regardless of what any employee handbook or stated policy formally says on the matter.

### What Selective Enforcement Actually Teaches

Every time the identical behavior produces a meaningfully different consequence depending entirely on who happened to do it, the organization is communicating something considerably more durable and consequential than just the specific incident itself. It's communicating that the stated rules are fundamentally conditional, and that the real operative rule is actually about who you are relative to whoever happens to be enforcing the standard in that moment. People don't simply distrust that one specific rule after watching it get applied unevenly a single time. They begin discounting the credibility of every other stated organizational standard as well, because the underlying mechanism that produced this particular inconsistency hasn't been demonstrated to be safely contained to just this one isolated case.

### The Pattern People Can Already Name

**The same behavior has reliably produced meaningfully different consequences for different people.** Not simply a matter of reasonable degree or context — a genuinely comparable situation that nonetheless produced a genuinely different formal outcome.

**People can describe the underlying pattern with real specificity when asked privately.** Ask quietly who the rules seem to bend for, and you'll consistently get a detailed, specific answer rather than a vague, unfocused sense of general unfairness.

**Nobody currently in a position to enforce standards consistently has actually acknowledged the pattern exists.** The inconsistency persists specifically because formally addressing it would mean directly confronting whoever has been quietly benefiting from it.

### A Reminder Is Not Evidence

Simply restating the existing policy — reminding everyone that the stated standard formally applies equally to all employees — doesn't meaningfully touch the actual underlying problem, because the written policy itself was never genuinely unclear to anyone. What people fundamentally don't currently believe is that it will actually be enforced evenly going forward, and a restated policy statement offers no real evidence one way or the other on that specific question. It's essentially a promise with no track record of follow-through behind it yet.

### Enforcement Against the Exception Is the Only Evidence That Counts

Rebuilding genuine trust in the standard requires visible, consistent enforcement going forward — including, and especially and specifically, against the particular person whose earlier exemption originally created the pattern people have noticed. That's unavoidably the harder version of the fix, because it usually means holding genuinely accountable someone whose protection from consequences is exactly why this became an established pattern rather than remaining a contained, one-off incident.

A single instance of genuinely even-handed enforcement against someone previously treated as exempt accomplishes more toward resetting the standard than any number of restated written policies ever could. The workforce, collectively, is watching for concrete evidence, not for additional language. Until they actually receive that evidence, the underlying pattern holds firm — regardless of whatever gets formally restated in the meantime.
