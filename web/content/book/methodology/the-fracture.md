# The Fracture

Two leaders at the top of this organization are in open conflict, and everyone below them already knows it, even if nobody's said so in a meeting. You navigate meetings differently depending on who's in the room: what you bring up, how you phrase it, which version of an update you give. Decisions require political calculation before they require judgment, and you've gotten good enough at that calculation that you barely notice yourself doing it anymore.

People have chosen sides, largely because the leaders already have, whether or not either of them would describe it that way out loud. The work that requires both of them to function, and there's more of it than either would probably admit, if pressed, is exactly the work that isn't getting done, quarter after quarter, while everyone waits for something to resolve.

---

### Two Leaders, Not One Team

Two senior leaders in open conflict looks, from a distance, like something the rest of the company can simply route around. It can't, not for long, and not without real cost. Every function that depends on both of them for sign-off, coordination, or shared strategic direction is now running through a relationship that's actively adversarial rather than merely imperfect. The organization isn't just losing the upside of their collaboration. It's paying an active, ongoing tax on everything that requires them to cooperate: timelines that stretch because approvals bounce between two people who aren't speaking productively, initiatives that get quietly shelved because nobody wants to chase both signatures.

### Everyone Below Them Is Also Paying

Choosing a side isn't usually a conscious or deliberate decision on anyone's part. It's a survival response that develops gradually: people figure out, through repeated observation, which leader's approval actually matters for their specific work and their advancement within the organization, and they orient toward that person whether or not they ever intended to pick a side. Once that sorting happens broadly enough, the organization isn't functioning as one team anymore, whatever the org chart continues to say. It's functioning as two, informally, reporting to leaders who aren't aligned on strategy, priorities, or often even the basic facts of a given situation.

The people genuinely caught in the middle, whose work structurally requires engagement from both leaders, carry a cost that's rarely tracked anywhere formal. Every project that needs sign-off from both sides moves at the speed of the worst recent day in that relationship, not at the speed the work itself would otherwise allow.

### The Two Calendars You're Actually Running

**Meetings run differently depending on who's present.** This is not a minor tonal shift you could imagine away. It's a real, substantive change in what gets said, what gets decided, and what quietly gets deferred until the other leader isn't in the room.

**Staff have identifiable loyalties, even when nobody states them out loud.** Ask, privately, who reports what to whom informally, off the official chart, and a consistent pattern emerges that doesn't match the documented reporting structure.

**Cross-functional work needing both leaders stalls disproportionately compared to everything else.** Compare timelines on initiatives that require joint sign-off against timelines on initiatives that don't. The gap between the two is the fracture, made visible in a way that's hard to argue with once you've actually looked.

### A Handshake in the Mediator's Office

Putting both leaders in a room with a mediator and securing an agreement to move forward professionally usually produces a genuinely sincere agreement in the moment, and one that doesn't hold past the next real disagreement, because it addressed the surface friction in the relationship without touching whatever structural condition keeps generating conflict between two people with overlapping authority and no clear tiebreaker above them. The agreement was never the hard part. Preventing the next version of the same conflict was, and a mediated conversation rarely reaches that layer.

### The Question Underneath the Conflict

Real resolution requires someone above both of them, or a board with the actual standing to act, to ask the structural question instead of continuing to treat this as a personal one: do these two roles genuinely need to share authority the way they currently do, given how consistently that shared authority has produced conflict rather than complementary strength? And if the conflict turns out to be genuinely irreconcilable at the personal level, which has to change: the relationship itself, through one or both leaders departing, or the structure that continues to force them together despite the evidence that it doesn't work?

That's a considerably harder conversation than arranging a mediated truce, because it usually means someone with real seniority loses scope, authority, or their role entirely. It's also the only version of resolution that doesn't simply reset the clock until the next disagreement arrives on schedule.
