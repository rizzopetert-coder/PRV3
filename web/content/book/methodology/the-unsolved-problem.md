# The Unsolved Problem

There was a complaint, a while back. It went through the right process — investigated, addressed, formally closed. Settled, documented, filed. By every record the organization keeps, the matter is resolved.

The person affected moved on, in whatever sense that's possible after something like this. The behavior that caused it didn't. It's still happening, in some form, to someone else, somewhere in this organization right now — because what got resolved was the complaint, the paperwork, the immediate disruption. The condition underneath it was never what anyone addressed.

---

## Closed Is Not the Same as Fixed

Once a matter is formally resolved, the instinct is to consider it over. That instinct is right about the administrative status and wrong about the underlying reality.

Resolution processes are built to address a specific complaint, by a specific person, about a specific incident. They are not built to ask whether the behavior, the structural gap, or the person at the center of it is likely to produce the next one. Closing the file answers the first question. The second question never gets asked — and most organizations file that silence as an answer.

---

## Why the Second Complaint Costs So Much More

The first complaint, handled reasonably, is a single incident with a single resolution. The second complaint about the same behavior is something else entirely.

Now there's a documented prior resolution in the file. That document doesn't just fail to help — it actively works against the organization. A second complaint with a closed prior file is no longer a story about an isolated incident. It's a story about an organization that knew, addressed it once, and let the condition continue anyway. The first resolution, instead of protecting the organization, becomes the clearest evidence that it already had the information it needed and didn't act on the root cause.

---

## What the Resolution Actually Resolved

Closing a complaint is not the same as addressing what caused it. A transfer moves the problem. A verbal warning documents that you noticed it. A mediated conversation gives both parties a chance to perform resolution. A policy reminder reminds people a policy exists. None of these things change the underlying condition. What would? Changing the person's role, authority, or access in a meaningful way. Restructuring the dynamic that allowed the behavior to persist. Removing the person entirely if the pattern is established. The organizations that actually resolve these complaints can point to something structural that's different afterward. Most can't.

The problem is structural. There's no step in most resolution processes that asks the one question that matters afterward: now that we know this happened once, what has the organization actually changed that would prevent it from happening again? Without that reflection, the file closes on a partial fix nobody flagged as partial.

---

## How to Tell It Wasn't Actually Resolved

**A complaint was formally raised, investigated, and closed.** Real record, real process, real resolution on paper.

**The condition that produced it is still structurally possible.** The person, the role, the dynamic, the gap in oversight — whatever generated the original complaint hasn't changed. Only the complaint has been closed.

**Nobody checked whether it recurred, until it did.** Nothing in the process prompts anyone to look proactively. The gap between resolution and recurrence is invisible until it isn't.

---

## Why Closing the File Felt Like Enough

This isn't usually negligence. Most people handling a complaint do exactly what the process asks of them, conscientiously. The process is designed to end at resolution. Closing the file is what success looks like, by the only definition available.

Nobody owns the question of whether a resolved complaint represents a contained incident or a pattern waiting on its next instance. That question lives in the space between HR, the manager, and whoever closed the file — and in that space, it belongs to no one.

---

## What a Real Resolution Adds

Every complaint resolution needs a second question that most processes skip: has the underlying condition actually changed, or has it just been quieted?

Answering it honestly takes one additional step at close: before the file is finalized, the person responsible for the resolution documents specifically what changed about the person, the role, the oversight, or the structure that makes this less likely to happen again. Not a general statement that the matter was addressed. A specific account of what is now different.

If nothing is different, that gets documented too, along with whatever monitoring or follow-up is now in place. A file that honestly records "the behavior was addressed but the underlying condition hasn't changed, and here's how we're watching for it" is a far stronger position than a file that simply says closed.

A second complaint about the same root cause is direct evidence the first resolution didn't reach it. Treat it that way — not as a recurrence to handle the same way, but as information that the response now needs to go further than it did the first time.

Quiet isn't the same as resolved, and the difference usually shows up at the worst possible time.
