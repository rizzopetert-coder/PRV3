# The Unreported Hazard

There's a safety concern in this organization right now. Physical, psychological, operational — the category matters less than the fact that it exists, people know about it, and it hasn't been formally reported.

That last part is the actual finding. Not the hazard itself. The fact that people who know about it haven't reported it is a separate, larger piece of information about what this organization has taught people to expect when they raise something. Somewhere, that calculation got made — consciously or not — and reporting lost.

---

## Two Problems, Not One

Fix the hazard and you've solved half the problem — the half that will come back in a different form next time.

The silence around the hazard is a fact about trust. Specifically, about what people believe happens when they raise something through the formal channel built to receive exactly this kind of concern. Fixing the hazard without addressing why nobody reported it leaves that belief fully intact. And that belief is what determines whether the next hazard gets caught early or gets discovered the same way this one was — by someone outside the formal system finally noticing.

---

## What the Calculation Actually Looks Like

Nobody decides not to report a hazard because they don't care about safety. The calculation is about consequence: will reporting this change anything, or will it create friction, attention, or personal risk with no real payoff on the other end?

That calculation runs on evidence. If people have watched previous reports go nowhere — or watched the person who raised something get quietly sidelined — the calculation updates. And it updates for everyone watching, not just the person it happened to directly. By the time a hazard goes unreported, the organization has usually already taught, through some prior episode, that silence is the rational response.

---

## Why the Clock Keeps Running

A hazard that's known but unreported doesn't wait. It continues accumulating exposure while nobody with authority to act knows it's there.

When it finally surfaces — usually because something goes wrong, not because someone reported it — the organization faces a harder question than the hazard itself: how long was this known, by how many people, and why did none of them think reporting was worth the risk? That question, once asked publicly, often does more damage than the original hazard would have if it had surfaced through the right channel the first time someone noticed it.

---

## What Marks This Condition

**People can describe the hazard specifically and consistently.** Not vague unease. Ask informally and you get a clear, concrete account of what the concern actually is.

**No formal report exists despite that awareness.** The gap between what people say informally and what's on record anywhere is the condition itself.

**There's a nameable reason reporting felt unsafe or pointless.** Almost always traceable to a prior episode — something someone watched happen, or something that happened to them directly, that taught the lesson currently being applied.

---

## Why Reminding People to Report Doesn't Fix It

The people not reporting already know the channels exist. What they don't believe is that using them produces a good outcome. A reminder doesn't touch that belief.

What changes the calculation is watching a report get a real, visible, appropriate response — not once, but as a pattern. Until that pattern exists and has been witnessed, every reminder to use the formal channel is competing against direct evidence that it doesn't work. Direct evidence wins.

---

## What Closing the Gap Actually Requires

Fix the hazard — that's urgent and non-negotiable. Then do the second thing most organizations skip: find out specifically why this one went unreported.

Not a general survey about psychological safety. A direct conversation with the people who knew and stayed quiet, asking what they expected to happen if they raised it, and what previous experience taught them that. The answer will point to something specific — a prior report that went nowhere, a person who got managed out for speaking up, a channel that exists on paper and doesn't function in practice.

Fix that thing. Then demonstrate the fix by responding visibly and appropriately to the next concern that comes through the formal channel — whatever it is. Do it again the next time. The belief that silence is safer changes slowly, and only through accumulated evidence that it isn't.

The hazard is the presenting problem. The silence is the one that will produce the next one.
