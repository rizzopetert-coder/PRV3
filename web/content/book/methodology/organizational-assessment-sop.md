## What This Document Is

This is the master SOP for a People Tactics and Strategy engagement scoped to organizational assessment. It covers the full engagement sequence, the judgment that connects the phases, and the circumstances that require a different approach. Each phase has its own dedicated SOP. This document is what you read before those — and what you return to when something in the engagement doesn't fit the expected pattern.

---

## The Engagement at a Glance

Five phases. Each is distinct. None can be compressed without cost.

**Intake** — Establish scope, access, and the leader's current picture of the organization.
**Investigation** — Interview, observe, and collect. Find what is actually there.
**Analysis** — Synthesize findings. Trace friction to origin. Identify patterns.
**Report** — Write the diagnosis. Structure the findings. Calibrate the artifact.
**Delivery** — Present the findings. Hold the room. Close the engagement.

The phases are sequential. Analysis cannot begin honestly until investigation is complete. The report cannot be written until analysis has produced a clear diagnosis. Practitioners who compress the sequence — moving toward conclusions before the investigation is finished, drafting the report while analysis is still open — tend to find that their early conclusions shape what they look for rather than the other way around. That is the error the methodology is designed to prevent.

---

## What Makes This Engagement Different

The practitioner conducting a People Tactics and Strategy assessment is not a consultant in the conventional sense. There is no ongoing relationship to protect, no follow-on engagement to position for, no organizational politics to navigate carefully. The assessment is the engagement. What that means in practice:

You are the only person in the room with nothing to lose from an honest finding. Every person you interview has something at stake in what you conclude. The leader who commissioned the engagement has a picture of the organization they have been living inside — and some part of that picture is wrong, or you would not be there. Your job is to find what is wrong with the picture without being captured by it.

That requires discipline in every phase. It is easiest to lose in intake, when the leader's framing of the problem is fresh and persuasive. It is hardest to recover from in the report, when a conclusion reached too early has already shaped the writing.

## Sequence Guidance

**Intake** establishes the container. You are learning what the leader believes is true, what they want to understand, and what access they are prepared to grant. You are not accepting their hypothesis. You are documenting it — because knowing what the leader expected to find is useful context when you find something different. Intake also sets the scope boundary. What is in and what is out needs to be explicit before investigation begins. Scope that drifts during investigation is a risk to the integrity of the findings.

**Investigation** is the core of the engagement. Interviews, observation, whatever documentary access the organization provides. The practitioner is looking for what is actually happening — not what people say is happening, which is different. Pay attention to what people don't say, what they say carefully, and what they say when they think you are not evaluating it. Patterns across interviews matter more than any single account. No finding from a single source should appear in the report without corroboration or explicit qualification.

**Analysis** is where investigation becomes diagnosis. The practitioner is not summarizing what they heard — they are identifying what it means. Where is the friction? What is sustaining it? What is its origin? Analysis requires sitting with ambiguity long enough to let the pattern emerge rather than imposing one. The temptation is to move quickly to conclusions. Resist it. The report will only be as good as the analysis behind it.

**Report** is the artifact of record. It says what was found, what it means, and what it implies. It is specific enough to be acted on and honest enough to be uncomfortable where discomfort is warranted. The report is not advocacy — it is diagnosis. It does not tell the organization what it wants to do. It tells the organization what is true and what that means for the people responsible for leading it.

**Delivery** is where the findings meet the person who commissioned them. This is not a presentation in the conventional sense. The report exists. The delivery is a conversation about what it contains. The practitioner's job in this phase is to hold the findings steady — to present them clearly, to answer questions honestly, and to resist the pressure to soften conclusions that land hard. How the leader receives the findings is information. It belongs in your own notes, not in the report.

## Circumstance Guidance

### When the leader who commissioned the engagement is part of the problem

This is common. It is not a reason to soften the findings. It is a reason to be precise about how they are framed. The report names behavior and its organizational consequences — not character. The distinction matters both for accuracy and for the leader's ability to receive the findings and act on them. A diagnosis the leader cannot hear is not useful. A diagnosis that obscures what is true to protect the leader's comfort is not honest. The practitioner holds both of those at once.

If the findings implicate the commissioning leader significantly, delivery requires particular care. Present the findings as written. Do not preview or soften them in advance of the conversation. Do not apologize for what the investigation found. The leader commissioned an honest assessment. This is what one looks like.

### When the organization is in active crisis

Active crisis changes the investigation conditions. People are frightened, alliances are shifting, and accounts of what is happening will be less reliable than usual — more self-protective, more politically shaped. The practitioner needs to weight interview data accordingly and look harder at observable conditions and documentary evidence. The report should note the crisis conditions explicitly, because they are context for the findings.

Do not allow the urgency of the crisis to compress the engagement sequence. An organization in crisis wants answers faster than the methodology can honestly produce them. The practitioner's job is to hold the sequence while acknowledging the urgency. A diagnosis reached quickly under pressure is not better than one reached carefully. It is just faster and more likely to be wrong.

### When access is restricted

Restricted access is a finding. If the practitioner cannot interview key people, cannot review relevant documents, or encounters systematic resistance to investigation, that pattern belongs in the report — not as a complaint about the engagement conditions, but as an organizational signal. Organizations that cannot be investigated honestly are telling you something about how they operate.

Name what was and was not accessible. Name what that means for the confidence level of the findings. Do not produce a diagnosis with false precision when the investigation was materially constrained.

### When key people won't talk

Different from restricted access. This is about individuals who are technically available but unwilling to engage honestly. It happens. Do not pressure. Do not pursue. Note it, look for corroboration through other channels, and qualify any finding that depends on information you could not obtain. An interview that produced nothing is still data — about the person, about the conditions they are operating in, or both.

### When the organization is early stage with a small leadership team

The investigation surface is smaller and the interdependencies are tighter. Findings about one person are harder to separate from findings about the organization because at this scale they may be the same thing. Be precise in the report about what is structural and what is individual. Early stage organizations often have friction that is a normal condition of their stage rather than a dysfunction — the report should distinguish between the two.

### When the organization is large and layered

The investigation requires deliberate sampling across levels. What leadership believes is happening and what is happening at the working level are often significantly different. Findings from a single organizational level are not findings about the organization — they are findings about that level. The report should be explicit about where in the organization each finding originates.

### When there is nothing significant to report

This happens. The investigation finds friction that is within normal operating range, nothing that rises to the level of a diagnosis, no pattern that requires intervention. The report says so — clearly, without hedging, and without manufacturing significance to justify the engagement. A clean bill of organizational health is a valuable finding. Treat it as one.

## What Good Execution Looks Like

The practitioner who executes this engagement well is not the one who produces the most thorough report or the most surprising findings. It is the one who found what was actually there, said so accurately, and delivered it in a way the organization could receive and act on.

Disarming and relentless, held simultaneously. The relentlessness is in the accuracy of the findings. The disarming is in how they are delivered. Neither quality works without the other.
