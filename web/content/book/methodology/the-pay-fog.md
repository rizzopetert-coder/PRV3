# The Pay Fog

You've compared notes with people in similar roles, carefully and more than once, and the numbers genuinely don't make sense. Same level, comparable scope, meaningfully different pay, and nobody in a position to explain it can produce an actual framework that accounts for the difference.

It wasn't a real problem, practically speaking, when those comparisons stayed private and informal. Pay transparency requirements are changing that quickly and in ways this organization clearly isn't prepared to defend once the question moves from private curiosity to a legally required disclosure.

---

### Privacy Wasn't a Fix. It Was Just Cover.

Compensation inconsistency doesn't actually disappear simply because nobody's comparing notes. It just doesn't cost the organization anything in the moment while comparison stays genuinely rare. Pay bands drift meaningfully over years through an accumulation of individually reasonable decisions: one negotiation handled generously because a candidate pushed back effectively, one hire made under real budget pressure that constrained the offer, one routine raise cycle skipped during a genuinely lean quarter for the business. None of those individual decisions were ever coordinated against any shared, consistent framework, largely because there often wasn't one rigorous enough to actually coordinate against in the first place.

### What Transparency Actually Changes

Pay transparency laws don't create the underlying inconsistency in compensation. That was already there, quietly, regardless of any disclosure requirement. What they do is remove the specific condition that allowed the inconsistency to persist genuinely unnoticed for years. Once pay ranges are legally posted and colleagues can openly and legally compare notes with each other, the same numbers that were quietly tolerated for years suddenly become an immediate and pointed question, and "there's no clean explanation available" is a considerably worse answer to have to give out loud, in a legally exposed context, than it ever was to simply leave the question unasked in private.

### Already in the Fog

**Employees in genuinely comparable roles have materially different pay with no defensible rationale anyone can articulate.** Not simply a modest, expected band width: an actual gap that nobody in HR or in leadership can currently explain with any real specificity.

**There's no consistent internal framework actually governing how pay decisions get made across the organization.** Individual manager discretion, individual negotiation outcomes, individual budget cycles, with no shared underlying logic meaningfully tying any of it together.

**People have already started comparing notes, whether formally or informally, and they're noticing the discrepancies.** Once this process genuinely starts among a workforce, it doesn't meaningfully reverse itself or fade back into private uncertainty.

### One Explanation at a Time, Forever

This particular person was hired during an unusually tight labor market. That person negotiated unusually hard during their offer. Each individual explanation might well be entirely true on its own terms. None of them, individually or even collectively across many cases, substitutes for an actual coherent framework. The next comparison an employee makes will simply surface the next unexplained gap, and the accumulating pattern of ad hoc, case-by-case explanations eventually becomes its own clear evidence that no real underlying system exists at all.

### What the Framework Has to Include

**A documented, consistent set of criteria for how pay actually gets set, genuinely tied to role, level, and current market data, not left to manager discretion alone.** This has to be real and specific enough to actually explain individual outcomes, not a vague statement of principle.

**A genuine retroactive audit against that new framework, run now, specifically to find where current pay already falls outside the band it reasonably should be in.** This audit will not be comfortable, and it shouldn't be designed to be.

**A concrete plan for closing whatever gaps the audit finds, prioritized honestly by size and by legal exposure, not simply addressed in whatever order employees happen to ask about their own situation first.**

Some portion of what that audit finds will genuinely be expensive to fix properly. It remains considerably less expensive to address now, on the organization's own chosen timeline, than it will be once pay transparency requirements or an individual employee's own comparison forces the exact same question into the open with no framework yet ready to answer it credibly.
