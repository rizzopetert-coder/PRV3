# The Culture That Wasn't

The all-hands presentation still talks about the culture here the way it would have been accurately described back in the company's earlier days — the founding story, the values everyone could once recite confidently, the specific thing that genuinely made this place feel meaningfully different from anywhere else. You remember clearly when that description was actually accurate. It isn't anymore, in any real sense, and everyone who's been here long enough already quietly knows it, whether or not anyone's said so out loud in a room where it would carry consequences.

New hires figure out the gap fairly fast, typically within their first ninety days on the job. The people who've been here the longest stopped genuinely expecting that gap to close a good while ago now.

---

### No One Decision Broke It

There's rarely a single identifiable moment anyone could reasonably point to as the actual break — culture drifting meaningfully away from how it's officially described isn't usually caused by any one clearly bad decision, but rather by a gradual accumulation of considerably smaller ones, each individually defensible enough on its own terms in the moment, that collectively moved the organization somewhere its founding description never actually anticipated or accounted for. Looking specifically for the single decision that caused all of this is looking for something that most likely doesn't actually exist as a discrete event. The accumulation itself, over time, is genuinely the cause.

### Why Maintaining the Old Description Gets Harder Every Year

The gap between the organization's officially stated culture and its actually lived reality doesn't remain static over time — it steadily widens, because every new consequential decision inevitably gets made against the organization's genuine current reality, not against its original founding description, and each one quietly adds another data point to the accumulating gap. Maintaining the increasingly outdated original language while the lived experience keeps meaningfully diverging further requires steadily increasing effort from whoever happens to be doing that maintenance, and eventually that visible effort itself becomes recognizable as performance rather than as accurate description.

### Where the Description Stops Matching

**The all-hands description of "who we genuinely are" doesn't actually match what people experience in their actual day-to-day work.** Ask someone relatively new what genuinely surprised them most during their first three months, and you'll usually hear some version of exactly this gap described back to you.

**Long-tenured employees have quietly stopped correcting the gap whenever they hear it repeated publicly.** Early on, in the organization's history, someone might have gently pushed back in a meeting when the description felt inaccurate. By the time this condition is genuinely entrenched, people simply let the description pass entirely without comment — not because they agree with it, but out of accumulated fatigue with correcting it.

**The specific people who cared most deeply about the original culture are consistently the ones leaving first.** They're typically the people best equipped to actually notice the widening gap clearly, and simultaneously the least willing to spend additional years of their career watching it continue to widen without meaningful change.

### A Better Pitch for the Same Description

The natural instinct, once the underlying drift is genuinely noticed by leadership, is to reinvest specifically in communicating the original stated values more clearly and more emphatically — a refreshed values presentation deck, a renewed emphasis at the next all-hands meeting. That effort doesn't actually touch the real underlying problem, because the original values were never genuinely poorly communicated in the first place. They were accurately and clearly communicated, and the organization subsequently simply stopped consistently living them out in practice. Better, clearer communication of a description that's become genuinely inaccurate just makes that inaccuracy more polished and more convincing on the surface.

### This Requires an Honest Choice, Not a Better Pitch

If the gap between the organization's stated culture and its actual lived culture is genuinely real, there are ultimately only two honest directions available to move in: either change the organization's actual day-to-day practices to meaningfully close the distance back toward what's officially described, or formally change the description itself to accurately match what the organization has genuinely become over time. Both of those directions are entirely legitimate choices to make. What isn't legitimate is continuing indefinitely to describe the organization as something it currently, demonstrably isn't — because every single person who notices that particular gap and chooses to say nothing about it is quietly and continuously recalibrating exactly how much they genuinely trust everything else the organization claims about itself going forward.
