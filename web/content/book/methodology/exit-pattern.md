Another one just put in notice.

You saw it coming, sort of. They'd been quieter lately. Less engaged in meetings. The LinkedIn activity uptick you noticed but didn't mention to anyone. And now here they are, two weeks notice, polite smile, something about an exciting opportunity they couldn't pass up.

HR will do the exit interview. The feedback will be professional and vague: "looking for growth," "ready for a new challenge," "really enjoyed my time here." You'll add it to the file with the other exit interviews that all sound weirdly similar. And then you'll move on to backfilling the role, hoping the next person sticks around longer.

But here's the thing that's been nagging at you, maybe for a while now: this isn't random. It's a pattern. Same type of person. Same tenure band. Same quiet, professional departure. Same polite fiction about why they're leaving.

The exit interviews aren't telling you the real story. The real story is what they say to their friends six months later, once they've got nothing to lose by being honest. The real story is the Glassdoor review they'll post from a throwaway account. The real story is the text thread with former colleagues where someone asks "how's it going over there?" and the response is just a gif of a dumpster fire.

You're not losing people randomly. You're losing people in a pattern. And the pattern is going to continue until the conditions that created it change.

---

## What the Pattern Tells You

Let's start with who's leaving.

Is it your best people? The ones you actually wanted to keep? The ones who had options and used them? That's a different signal than losing people who were never going to work out anyway. If your high performers are walking at a higher rate than your average performers, that's not bad luck. That's feedback.

Is it a specific team or function? Same manager losing people every eighteen months? Same department churning through senior hires? The pattern is localized, which means the cause is probably localized too. Someone in that system is the reason people leave, and everyone who's left knows it even if you don't.

Is it a tenure band? People bailing at the one-year mark? At three years? There's usually a moment when the gap between expectations and reality becomes undeniable. The timing of departures tells you when people hit that moment in your organization.

The pattern is data. It's better data than the engagement survey, honestly. People will lie to HR. They won't lie with their feet.

---

## The Story Behind the Story

Nobody tells the truth in an exit interview. Or rather, they tell a version of the truth that's been carefully edited for professional consequences.

"I found a great opportunity" means "I went looking because something here wasn't working."

"Ready for a new challenge" means "I stopped being challenged here, or I got tired of challenges that weren't worth it."

"It was a hard decision" means "It actually wasn't that hard once I got the offer."

The real story usually involves something the person decided they couldn't change. A manager they gave up on. A culture that wouldn't budge. A promotion path that was blocked by someone who wasn't going anywhere. A promise that was made and quietly broken. A realization that the things they cared about weren't the things the organization actually rewarded.

You'll hear this story if you ask them six months later, over coffee, once they're settled somewhere else and have no reason to manage the relationship anymore. They'll tell you what they wished they could have said on the way out. And you'll realize they were never going to say it while they still worked there, because they'd already learned that honesty wasn't safe.

---

## Why Retention Programs Miss

The standard response to an exit pattern is a retention program. More competitive comp. Better benefits. Stay bonuses for key people. Career development initiatives. Maybe an engagement survey to find out what people want.

These things aren't useless. But they miss the actual problem most of the time.

People don't leave for five percent more money. They leave because something broke: their relationship with their manager, their belief in the direction, their sense that they could have an impact, their trust that the organization would do the right thing. The money is just the permission slip. The recruiter call is just the catalyst. The actual cause is almost always relational or cultural, and it almost always happened months before the resignation.

Retention programs treat the symptom. The symptom is that people are leaving. The cause is that something in the environment made leaving feel like the right choice.

You can't comp your way out of a bad manager. You can't benefits your way out of a broken culture. You can't career-path your way out of an organization that keeps promoting the wrong people.

---

## The Uncomfortable Truth

Here's the part nobody wants to hear: the pattern is telling you something true about your organization, and you might not like what it's saying.

Maybe the culture isn't as healthy as the leadership team believes. Maybe the manager everyone defends is actually the reason people keep leaving. Maybe the strategy that seemed so clear to the executives looks incoherent from two levels down. Maybe the values on the wall have diverged from the values in practice, and the people who care most about values are the ones walking out the door.

The exit pattern is a signal. Ignoring it doesn't make it go away. It just means the next cohort of departures will have the same story as the last cohort, and you'll still be doing the same exit interviews, hearing the same polite non-answers, wondering why you can't seem to hold onto the people you most want to keep.

The organizations that break the pattern are the ones willing to ask: *What would have to change for the next person in this role to have a materially different experience than the people who left?*

That question leads to uncomfortable places. It leads to conversations about specific people and specific decisions. It leads to admitting that something isn't working. It leads to change that's harder than just throwing money at the problem.

But it's the only question that actually matters.

---

## What the Pattern Costs You

Let's do the math you've been avoiding.

Every departure costs you the recruiting effort to replace them. The ramp time for the new person. The productivity gap in between. The institutional knowledge that walked out the door. The relationships with clients or partners that now have to be rebuilt. The impact on the team that's left behind, who are now absorbing extra work and wondering if they should start looking too.

Multiply that by the number of departures. Add the ones you're going to have over the next year if nothing changes. Add the cost of the people who are still there but have mentally checked out: the ones who are giving you seventy percent because they've concluded this place isn't worth a hundred.

Now compare that number to the cost of addressing whatever's actually causing the pattern.

The math usually makes the decision obvious. The hard part is admitting that the math applies to you.

---

## The Question We Ask

When an organization tells us they have a retention problem, we don't start with compensation benchmarking or engagement surveys. We start with a question.

*What would the people who left say if they were being completely honest about why they left?*

Sometimes the leadership team knows. They've heard the real feedback through back channels. They know exactly what's driving people out. They've just been hoping they could fix it quietly, or that it would fix itself, or that maybe it wasn't as bad as the departures suggested.

Sometimes they don't know. The feedback loop is broken. The truth is being filtered out somewhere between the people who are leaving and the people who could do something about it.

Either way, that's where the work starts. Not with programs. Not with initiatives. With the honest answer to a simple question.

What's actually causing people to leave?

And what are you going to do about it?

If you're ready to stop losing the people you want to keep, we should talk.
