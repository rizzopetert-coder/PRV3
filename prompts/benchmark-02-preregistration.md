## PX1: Family-owned wealth management firm

- **Observer's view:** A quiet office of cubicles where people keep to themselves and avoid eye contact. About thirty people in a family-owned wealth management firm, split between high-earning producers and the administrative staff who support them and manage the phones, all under one leadership group, with different cultural expectations on each side. Turnover sits in the admin staff, who were recruited on the promise of working around money and a path to growth, and instead end up buried in administrative work with no real route into the lucrative careers; on the producer side, only entry-level and early-career people leave. In meetings, a male producer usually does the talking, pushback is rare, and the culture expects compliance — challenge is tolerated only if it is exactly right.
- **What the leader believes:** Answering as the COO (managing director), who works alongside the owning family. Believes the turnover in admin and early-career roles isn't a real problem: those people are expendable, and the churn is partly the job market and partly the nature of the work. "You eat what you kill" is how the business runs, not something to fix. Holds an old-school view of balance, loyalty, compliance, and advancement.

## PX2: Suburban Massachusetts YMCA

- **Observer's view:** A YMCA of about 100 employees, mostly non-exempt, with a balanced mix of young and older, full-time and part-time staff. The lobby is busy, the front desk is friendly and multitasking, and kids move in and out of programs; the building is active but not pristine, and the halls crowd with after-school groups and open gym players. There is a clear process and escalation path, and serious issues go to salaried directors and executives, who document and handle them. Leadership meetings open with something warm, follow an organized agenda, and the executive invites everyone's updates. The real hallway talk happens late in the evening in empty offices, mostly complaints about workload or petty issues, and shared struggle is how relationships form. People leave over burnout, low pay, low recognition, slow career mobility, and culture change, with no concentration by program, director, or role; the people who get ahead are the ones whose success looks effortless — high-quality programs, satisfied clients and staff, no visible problems — with a lot of extra time and effort behind it.
- **What the leader believes:** Answering as the CHRO. A demanding environment with at times too many initiatives going. There is pressure on relatively young, relatively inexperienced staff to perform with limited resources and high stakes. It's not for everyone, and sometimes it is a challenge sustaining a strong work culture that also sets clear and high expectations. Engagement and retention of young up-and-comers can be challenging, especially in periods with really strong teams, because there is not enough opportunity for all of them; most people do not want to work in demanding nonprofit program work indefinitely and eventually want advancement to leadership roles. People processes, recognition programs, performance management, and management training matter more here than in for-profits because retention and development resources are limited.

- **Narrative (private view):** "Operating a 100-employee community organization like our suburban Massachusetts YMCA means we run almost entirely on the goodwill and energy of our people. What is negatively impacting our employees and members and the most glaring issue for us is the quiet exhaustion among our most dedicated full-time program directors. Because they care so deeply about the community and our mission, they are burning themselves out to keep everything running. They mask the strain because they don't want to let the community down, which means we don't see the crisis until it's too late and a beloved director hands in a sudden resignation. Compounding this is how stretched our department leaders are. We've expanded our programming and facility hours to meet the community's demand. As a result, critical staff development and strategic planning have been replaced by rushed updates in the hallways. The front desk, the childcare team, and the fitness staff end up operating in their own bubbles. A member gets one answer about a summer camp schedule from the membership desk and a completely different experience when they actually show up to drop off their kid. It feels like we've inadvertently lowered our baseline for performance. We find ourselves tolerating unreliability or poor work ethic simply because we desperately need the bodies on the floor to maintain legal ratios in our programs."
